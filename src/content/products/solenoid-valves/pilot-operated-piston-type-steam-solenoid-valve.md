faqs:
  - question: Why a piston instead of a diaphragm on steam?
    answer: A metal piston keeps its shape and strength at steam temperatures for far longer than an elastomer diaphragm, and it copes better with wet steam and the occasional slug of condensate.
  - question: Does a steam solenoid valve need a special coil?
    answer: Yes. Heat from the line travels up into the coil, so steam valves use coils with a high-temperature insulation class. An ordinary coil on a steam valve has a much shorter life.
  - question: Will it shut off steam with no pressure in the line?
    answer: No. Like other pilot operated valves it needs pressure in the line to open and close reliably. For low-pressure steam, the steam version of a semi-lift valve is often the better fit.
  - question: How should it be installed?
    answer: In a horizontal pipe with the coil on top, with condensate drained away before it reaches the valve and a strainer upstream. Leave room above to lift the coil off for servicing.

Steam is hard on rubber. A diaphragm that lasts for years on cold water can harden, crack or blister on steam. A piston type steam solenoid valve gets around this by replacing the diaphragm with a close-fitting metal piston, sealed with rings that tolerate the heat.

It works on the same pilot principle as a diaphragm valve. Inlet pressure reaches the space above the piston and holds it on the seat. The coil opens a small pilot port, the space above vents to the outlet, and pressure from below drives the piston up. When the coil drops out, the pilot closes and the piston returns to shut the valve.

Being pilot operated, it needs some pressure difference to open, which a steam main normally provides. A strainer upstream and good condensate drainage ahead of it go a long way towards a long service life.
