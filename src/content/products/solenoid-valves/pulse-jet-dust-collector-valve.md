faqs:
  - question: What does the sequencer or timer do?
    answer: It fires the valves one after another at set intervals, so only one row of bags is cleaned at a time while the collector keeps running. Cleaning can also be triggered by the rise in pressure drop across the bags.
  - question: Why does a pulse valve leak air all the time?
    answer: The usual causes are a torn or hardened diaphragm, dirt in the bleed hole, or a pilot that stays open. Water and oil in the compressed air speed up wear, so a dryer and filter on the header pay for themselves.
  - question: Integral solenoid or remote pilot?
    answer: An integral solenoid sits on the valve itself, which keeps the wiring simple. A remote pilot moves the coils into a separate enclosure away from the heat, dust and vibration on top of the collector, linked to each valve by a small air tube.

In a pulse jet dust collector, dusty air passes through rows of filter bags or cartridges, and the dust builds up on their outer surface. To clean them, a blowpipe above each row fires a short, sharp burst of compressed air down inside the bags. The shock flexes the fabric and the dust cake drops into the hopper below.

The pulse valve makes that burst. It is a large diaphragm valve mounted on the compressed air header, with a solenoid pilot on top or a remote pilot fed from a separate solenoid box. A timer or sequencer energises each valve for a fraction of a second in turn. The diaphragm snaps open, releases a slug of air into the blowpipe and reseats as soon as the pilot closes.

Pulse valves are made in right-angle and straight-through bodies, with threaded or compression-nut connections, to suit the layout of the header.
