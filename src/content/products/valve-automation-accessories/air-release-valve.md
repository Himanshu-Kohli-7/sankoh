  - question: Where should air release valves go?
    answer: At every high point, at the ends of long rising or falling runs, and just downstream of pumps. Fit an isolating valve beneath each one so it can be serviced without draining the main.
  - question: Single orifice or double orifice?
    answer: A single small orifice copes with air that gathers while the line is running. A double-orifice valve also moves large volumes of air during filling and draining, so it is the usual choice on mains that are emptied for maintenance.
  - question: Why is my air valve spitting water?
    answer: Usually dirt on the seat or a worn seal stops the float from sealing fully. Cleaning or replacing the float and seat normally puts it right.

Air gets into water pipelines when they are filled, through pump suction, and as dissolved air comes out of solution. It collects at high points, where it narrows the bore, raises pumping costs, upsets flow meters and can set off damaging pressure surges when a pocket moves suddenly.

An air release valve sits at those high points. Inside the body is a float. While the body is full of water, the float holds the small outlet shut. As air gathers, the water level inside falls, the float drops and the air vents to atmosphere. Rising water then lifts the float and closes the valve again.

Single-orifice valves release small amounts of air while the line is under pressure. Double-orifice, or combination, valves add a large orifice that discharges air quickly while a line fills and lets air back in as it drains, which protects the pipe from vacuum.
