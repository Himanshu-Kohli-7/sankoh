faqs:
  - question: Can a pneumatic butterfly valve control flow as well as isolate?
    answer: It can, once a positioner is added so the actuator holds the disc part-way open. Control is best through the middle of the stroke. Near the closed position the flow changes sharply with small movements, so the valve should be sized to work mostly between those extremes.
  - question: Can a wafer butterfly valve be used at the end of a line?
    answer: Generally not. A wafer body is held in place by the bolts running between two flanges, so it needs pipe on both sides. For a valve that closes off a dead end or a line that is opened for maintenance, a lug-type body is the usual choice.
  - question: How is the actuator size worked out?
    answer: From the torque the valve needs to break away from the seat, which depends on its size, the seat material and the pressure across it when it opens, and from the air pressure available. Give us the line pressure and your air pressure and we will match the two.

    answer: Yes. We repair butterfly valves and the actuators on them, of any make, from worn seats and damaged discs to actuators that stick or leak air. Most come back from our workshop in 2–3 days.

A pneumatic wafer butterfly valve is a butterfly valve whose hand lever has been replaced by a compressed-air actuator. A flat disc on a central shaft sits across the bore. When the actuator turns the shaft a quarter turn, the disc swings edge-on to the flow and the line opens; turned back, the disc closes against a soft seat lining the body.

"Wafer" describes the body: a slim ring that sits between the two pipe flanges and is gripped by the flange bolts. That keeps the valve short and light on large lines. The actuator bolts to a mounting pad on top of the valve, so the drive and the valve body can be serviced separately.

Air reaches the actuator through a [solenoid valve](/products/solenoid-valves/) controlled from the panel. A [limit switch box](/products/valve-automation-accessories/) on top confirms open and closed, which lets the control system check that the valve has really moved.
