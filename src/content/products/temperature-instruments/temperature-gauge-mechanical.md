faqs:
  - question: Which stem length should I order?
    answer: Long enough for the sensitive part of the stem to sit well inside the flowing fluid, usually around the middle third of the pipe on smaller lines. Too short and it reads the cooler pipe wall. Tell us the line size and how the tapping is made.
  - question: Should the stem enter from the bottom or the back?
    answer: Bottom entry suits a gauge on a vertical tapping, such as the top of a horizontal pipe. Back entry suits a horizontal tapping, such as the side of a tank or panel. Adjustable-angle versions cover awkward spots.
  - question: Can I fit the gauge straight into the pipe?
    answer: Only on low-pressure, non-critical duty. On most lines it goes into a thermowell, which takes the pressure and lets the gauge be swapped while the line stays live.
  - question: How accurate is a dial temperature gauge?
    answer: Good enough for operators to see whether a process is running normally, but less precise than an electronic sensor. Where a value feeds control or quality records, pair the gauge with an RTD and transmitter.

A mechanical temperature gauge, or dial thermometer, gives a direct reading of process temperature with nothing to wire and no power to supply. Operators use it to check at a glance that a line, tank or heat exchanger is running at the right heat.

The common bimetal type has a strip of two metals, bonded together and wound into a helix inside the stem. The two metals expand by different amounts as they warm, so the coil twists and turns the pointer. Gas- or liquid-filled types work differently: a sensing bulb, capillary and Bourdon element form one sealed system, and the fill expands with heat to move the pointer. A capillary lets the dial sit some distance from the bulb.

The stem is usually fitted inside a thermowell, which carries the line pressure and lets the gauge come out for checking without draining anything.
