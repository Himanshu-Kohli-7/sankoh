faqs:
  - question: What is the dead band, or differential, of a pressure switch?
    answer: It is the gap between the pressure at which the switch operates and the pressure at which it resets. Without it, the contact would chatter on and off around the set point. On a pump, a wider gap means fewer starts per hour.
  - question: Should I use a pressure switch or a transmitter?
    answer: A switch is the simpler, cheaper answer when you only need on/off action at one or two points. A transmitter suits loops where the pressure itself must be seen, recorded or controlled across its range.
  - question: Can a pressure switch be used on steam?
    answer: Yes, with a siphon in front of it so the switch element is kept away from live steam, just as with a gauge.
  - question: Is the contact rated to switch a motor directly?
    answer: Usually it is not meant to. Most switches should drive a contactor or relay, or a PLC input, rather than carry motor current. Tell us what the contact has to switch.

A pressure switch turns a pressure into an on/off decision. Inside, the process pressure acts on a diaphragm, piston or Bourdon element working against an adjustable spring. When the pressure overcomes the spring at the set point, the element trips a snap-action contact. When the pressure falls back by a set amount, the contact resets.

That simple action runs a great deal of plant equipment. A pump can start when the pressure falls and stop when it builds up again. A compressor unloads when its receiver is full. A machine shuts down if its oil pressure is lost.

Mechanical switches need no power to sense, only to carry the switched circuit. Electronic pressure switches add a display and adjustable points, often with an analogue output alongside. Differential versions compare two pressures, for example across a filter.
