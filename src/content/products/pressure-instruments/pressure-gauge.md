faqs:
  - question: Why are some gauges filled with liquid?
    answer: The filling, often glycerine or silicone oil, damps the movement so the pointer stays readable on pulsating or vibrating lines, and it lubricates the mechanism. It also keeps moisture from condensing inside the case.
  - question: What range should I choose?
    answer: One where the normal working pressure sits roughly in the middle third of the dial. A gauge that runs near its full scale all day wears quickly and has little margin for surges.
  - question: When is a diaphragm seal needed?
    answer: When the media would corrode, clog or set inside the Bourdon tube, as with slurries, viscous products or aggressive chemicals. The seal keeps the process away from the gauge and passes the pressure through a fill fluid.
  - question: Should every gauge on a steam line have a siphon?
    answer: Yes. Without one, live steam reaches the element and the heat damages the gauge and spoils its accuracy.

A pressure gauge gives a direct, local reading of the pressure in a pipe, vessel or machine, with no power supply or wiring. It is the instrument operators glance at most often, and usually the first sign that a pump, filter or regulator is not behaving.

Most dial gauges use a Bourdon tube, a flattened tube bent into a curve and sealed at its free end. Pressure inside it makes the tube try to straighten. A linkage and small gear segment turn that tiny movement into the sweep of a pointer across the dial. Diaphragm and capsule elements do the same job for low pressures.

Fit the gauge with an isolating [needle valve](/products/manual-valves/needle-valve/), a [siphon](/products/pressure-instruments/siphon-u-q-type/) on steam, and a liquid-filled case where the pointer would otherwise shake.
