  - question: What is the manifold under a DP transmitter for?
    answer: A three-valve or five-valve manifold lets the transmitter be isolated, equalised and vented without disturbing the process. Equalising both sides is also how the zero is checked in place.
  - question: How does a DP transmitter measure flow?
    answer: A restriction in the pipe, such as an orifice plate, creates a pressure drop that grows with the flow. The transmitter measures that drop and works out the flow from its square root.
  - question: Can one side be left open to the atmosphere?

    answer: Many DP transmitters are designed to survive full line pressure on one side, but the zero can still shift afterwards. Open and close the manifold valves in the right order to avoid it.

A differential pressure transmitter, often shortened to DP transmitter, has two process connections, high and low, and measures the difference between them. That one measurement answers a surprising number of plant questions.

Inside the sensing body, a diaphragm sits between the two pressures, usually behind fill fluid. It moves slightly towards the lower side, and the sensor turns that movement into a signal proportional to the difference. Because both sides sit at line pressure, it can resolve a small difference even on a high-pressure pipe.

Across an orifice plate, the difference gives flow. Between the top and bottom of a closed vessel, it gives level. Across a filter, it shows how far the element has clogged. Impulse lines carry the two pressures to the transmitter, normally through a valve manifold for isolation and zero checks.
