faqs:
  - question: What is the difference between an L-port and a T-port ball?
    answer: An L-port ball joins the common port to one of the other two, so it diverts flow one way or the other, and most can also shut off. A T-port ball can join all three ports at once, which lets it mix or split flow as well as divert, but it usually cannot shut all ports.
  - question: How do I say which flow pattern I need?
    answer: Sketch the three ports and mark which should be connected in each handle position. Flow patterns are easy to confuse in words, and the ball, the stops and the handle are all set to match the pattern.
  - question: Can one 3-way valve replace two 2-way valves?
    answer: Often, yes. For a simple changeover it saves a valve, a tee and space, and both lines cannot be shut by mistake. Two separate valves are still better where each line needs its own tight isolation.

A 3-way ball valve does the work of two ordinary valves and a tee. The body has three ports and the ball is drilled with an L-shaped or a T-shaped passage. Turning the handle a quarter or half turn lines the passage up with different pairs of ports, so the flow changes direction with no chance of both routes being closed at once.

With an L-port the valve acts as a selector: the common port feeds either the left or the right outlet. With a T-port it can also connect all three ports together, which is how it mixes two streams or splits one.

The handle or actuator position shows which ports are open, and stops limit the travel to the positions that the flow pattern allows. Because patterns vary between designs, it is worth settling the pattern on a sketch before ordering. For plain isolation, a standard [screwed](/products/manual-valves/ball-valve-screwed-end/) or flanged ball valve is the simpler choice.
