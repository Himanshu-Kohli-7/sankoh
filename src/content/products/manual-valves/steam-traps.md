faqs:
  - question: What are the main types of steam trap?
    answer: There are three families. Mechanical traps, such as ball float and inverted bucket traps, work on the difference in density between steam and water. Thermostatic traps react to temperature. Thermodynamic traps use the difference in the way steam and condensate flow under a disc.
  - question: Which type of steam trap do I need?
    answer: It depends on the application. Process equipment that must drain condensate as fast as it forms usually gets a float trap. Steam mains and tracing lines often use thermodynamic or thermostatic traps. Tell us the application, the steam pressure and the condensate load.
  - question: How can I tell that a steam trap has failed?
    answer: A trap fails open or closed. Failed open, it blows live steam and wastes fuel. Failed closed, condensate backs up, the equipment heats poorly and water hammer can follow. A sight glass after the trap makes its discharge easy to check.
  - question: Should a strainer be fitted before a steam trap?
    answer: Yes, unless the trap has one built in. Scale and dirt are the most common cause of trap failure.

A steam trap is an automatic valve that removes condensate, the water that forms as steam gives up its heat, without letting live steam escape. It also releases air. The trap opens when condensate reaches it and closes when steam arrives, with no operator or outside power.

Traps matter because water in a steam system does harm. It coats heating surfaces and slows the process, and fast-moving steam can pick it up and throw it against bends and valves as water hammer. A trap that leaks steam is just as costly, in wasted fuel.

There are three main families of trap: mechanical, thermostatic and thermodynamic. Each suits different duties, so the choice depends on the equipment being drained, the steam pressure and the amount of condensate. Fit a [strainer](/products/manual-valves/strainer/) ahead of the trap to keep dirt out of it.
