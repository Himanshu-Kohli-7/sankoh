  - question: What is the difference between a wafer and a lug butterfly valve?
    answer: A wafer valve is clamped between two pipe flanges by long bolts that pass around its body. A lug valve has threaded lugs, so each flange bolts to the valve separately. That helps when the pipe on one side has to come off for maintenance while the valve stays in place.
  - question: Can a butterfly valve be used to regulate flow?
    answer: Yes, within limits. A butterfly valve gives reasonable control through the middle of its travel, but it is not a precise throttling valve and should not be left almost closed for long. For fine control, a globe valve is the better choice.
  - question: Should I choose a hand lever or a gear operator?
    answer: A hand lever is quick and suits smaller valves. Larger valves need more torque, so they are fitted with a gear operator and handwheel. The gear also closes the valve slowly, which helps to avoid water hammer.

    answer: Often, yes. Most butterfly valves have a top flange that takes a pneumatic or electric actuator in place of the lever. We also supply butterfly valves already fitted with actuators.

A butterfly valve is a quarter-turn valve that controls flow with a disc mounted on a shaft across the pipe. Turn the lever a quarter turn and the disc swings from edge-on to the flow, which is open, to face-on, which is shut. A seat around the inside of the body seals against the edge of the disc.

Because the body is little wider than the disc, a butterfly valve is short, light and economical next to a ball or gate valve of the same size. That is why it is the usual choice on larger pipe sizes. The disc stays in the flow when the valve is open, so a little pressure is lost across it.

Most butterfly valves are wafer or lug pattern and fit between two pipe flanges. Smaller valves have a hand lever that locks in a notched plate. Larger ones use a gear operator with a handwheel.
