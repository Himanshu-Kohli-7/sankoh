faqs:
  - question: Plug valve or ball valve, which should I choose?
    answer: Both shut off in a quarter turn. A ball valve seals on two small seat rings and leaves a cavity around the ball. A plug valve seals over a large area of the plug and has no cavity, so it copes better with slurries, sticky products and liquids that solidify, at the cost of a higher turning torque.
  - question: What is the difference between lubricated and sleeved plug valves?
    answer: A lubricated valve has sealant injected between a metal plug and the body, which seals the valve and eases turning. It suits gas and oil and needs sealant topped up from time to time. A sleeved valve has a PTFE sleeve around the plug, needs no lubricant and suits chemicals.
  - question: Why has my plug valve become hard to turn?
    answer: On a lubricated valve the sealant has usually dried out or washed away, and fresh sealant often frees it. On a sleeved valve, product build-up or a damaged sleeve is the likely cause. A valve that has seized can usually be stripped and repaired.
  - question: Can a plug valve be used for throttling?
    answer: Only for coarse regulation. Special plug shapes exist for control duty, but a standard plug valve is an on/off valve, and running it part open for long wears the sealing surfaces.

A plug valve is one of the oldest valve designs and still one of the simplest. A plug with a port through it sits in a matching body. Turned one way, the port lines up with the pipe and flow passes straight through. A quarter turn later, the solid side of the plug blocks the line.

Because the plug fills the body, there is nowhere for product to sit. That makes the valve a good fit for gas distribution, where reliable tight shut-off matters, and for slurries, tars and crystallising liquids that would pack the cavity of other valves.

How the plug seals depends on the type: a film of sealant in a lubricated valve, a PTFE sleeve in a sleeved valve, or a moulded fluoropolymer lining in a lined valve for the most corrosive duties.
