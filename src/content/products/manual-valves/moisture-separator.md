faqs:
  - question: Why does wet steam matter if the steam trap removes condensate?
    answer: A trap drains water that has already collected at a low point. Droplets carried along in the steam flow pass straight by it. They wet the heating surface, cut heat transfer and erode valve seats and pipe bends. A separator takes them out of the flow first.
  - question: What drains the separator?
    answer: A steam trap on the bottom connection, usually a ball float trap because the water arrives in bursts and has to be cleared at once. On compressed air, an automatic drain valve or a manual drain cock does the same job.

A moisture separator removes the water droplets that travel along with steam or compressed air. Steam leaving a boiler is rarely perfectly dry, and more water forms as the pipework loses heat. Compressed air leaves the receiver still carrying condensed moisture and oil mist.

Inside the separator, baffles or vanes force the flow to turn sharply, and the larger body slows it down. The droplets are heavier than the gas, so they cannot follow the turn. They strike the baffles, run down the walls and gather in the bottom, where a trap or drain discharges them. The dried steam or air carries on through the outlet.

It has no moving parts of its own, and the drain trap does the only work. A separator is typically placed ahead of equipment that needs dry steam and ahead of control valves. It works together with the [steam traps](/products/manual-valves/steam-traps/) along the main, and fit a [strainer](/products/manual-valves/strainer/) ahead of its drain trap.
