faqs:
  - question: What is the difference between a weir type and a straight-through diaphragm valve?
    answer: A weir type has a raised saddle in the body that the diaphragm seals against. It needs only a short stroke, so the diaphragm lasts longer, and it throttles well. A straight-through type has a clear bore that suits slurries and viscous fluids, but it flexes the diaphragm further.
  - question: Why choose a diaphragm valve for corrosive chemicals?
    answer: There is no gland to leak and no metal working part in the fluid. Only the body lining and the diaphragm are wetted, and both can be chosen to resist the chemical, for example a rubber or PFA lined body with a PTFE-faced diaphragm.
  - question: How often does the diaphragm need changing?
    answer: It depends on temperature, pressure, the chemical and how often the valve is operated. The diaphragm is the wearing part and is changed from the top without taking the body out of the line, so it is worth keeping a spare.

A diaphragm valve has three main parts: a body, a flexible diaphragm and a bonnet. Turning the handwheel drives a compressor down onto the diaphragm, which presses against the body to shut off the flow. Turning it back lifts the diaphragm clear.

The diaphragm also seals the bonnet from the line. Nothing in the bonnet touches the fluid, there is no stem packing to leak, and the wetted surfaces are smooth with no pockets where product can lodge. That is why the design is common on corrosive chemicals, abrasive slurries and clean pharmaceutical duties, which are otherwise very different jobs.

Its limits are set by the diaphragm material. Temperature and pressure ratings are modest compared with a metal-seated valve, and they fall as size goes up, so check both for your duty. Where solids are heavy and the line is large, a [knife gate valve](/products/manual-valves/knife-gate-valve/) is the usual alternative.
