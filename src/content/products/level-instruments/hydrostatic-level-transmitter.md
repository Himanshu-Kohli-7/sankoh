faqs:
  - question: Why does the cable have a vent tube?
    answer: The sensor measures the pressure of the liquid plus the atmosphere pressing on its surface. A thin tube inside the cable lets atmospheric pressure reach the back of the sensor, so only the liquid head is measured. The open end must be kept dry, usually in a junction box with a moisture filter.
  - question: Does the liquid's density matter?
    answer: Yes. The transmitter measures pressure, and pressure for a given depth depends on density. A unit ranged for water will read wrongly in a heavier or lighter liquid unless it is scaled for it, and a density that changes with temperature or concentration adds error.
  - question: When is hydrostatic better than ultrasonic or radar?
    answer: In deep, narrow places such as borewells, where a beam from the top cannot work, and wherever foam, vapour or obstructions would upset a non-contact sensor. It is also economical. Non-contact types win where the liquid is corrosive, sticky or likely to build up on a probe.
  - question: How is the probe installed in a sump?
    answer: Lower it on its cable to just above the floor, clear of silt, and away from the inflow and the pump suction where turbulence disturbs the reading. In sewage, a probe with a large flush diaphragm resists clogging.

A hydrostatic level transmitter uses a simple fact: the deeper you go in a liquid, the higher the pressure. A pressure sensor placed at the bottom of a tank, well or sump feels the weight of the column above it, and the transmitter converts that pressure into a level signal.

In its most common form it is a slim stainless steel probe hanging on a cable. The cable carries the signal and the supply, and holds a vent tube that references the sensor to the atmosphere. Lowering a probe into a borewell takes minutes and needs no fittings on the well, which is why this type is the standard for groundwater. The alternative is a transmitter flanged or screwed to the outside of a tank near the bottom.

Nothing moves, and foam, vapour or internal pipework make no difference to the reading. The limits are that the sensor is in the liquid, so its materials must suit it, and that the reading depends on density.
