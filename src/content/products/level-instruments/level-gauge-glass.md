faqs:
  - question: What is the difference between tubular, reflex and transparent gauges?
    answer: A tubular gauge is a plain glass tube between two cocks, for low pressure and non-hazardous liquids. A reflex gauge has thick flat glass with prisms cut in the back, so the liquid shows dark and the space above shows bright, and it takes much higher pressure. A transparent gauge has flat glass front and back, so you can see through it to judge colour or an interface.
  - question: Which type is used on boilers?
    answer: Reflex gauges are the common choice for steam and water because the level stands out clearly. On high-pressure boilers, transparent gauges with mica shields and a lamp behind are used, since hot water attacks unprotected glass.
  - question: What do the ball checks in the gauge cocks do?
    answer: If the glass breaks, the rush of fluid carries a small ball onto a seat in each cock and cuts off the flow. They are a safety feature for pressurised or hazardous contents and are worth asking for.
  - question: How is a gauge glass kept readable?
    answer: Blow it down through the drain cock regularly to clear sediment, and replace the glass and gaskets when the glass turns cloudy or thin. Always isolate both cocks and release the pressure before opening it.

A level gauge glass is the most direct level instrument there is: you look at it and see the liquid. The gauge is connected to the vessel at the top and bottom, so the liquid inside it stands at the same height as in the vessel. Isolating cocks at each end allow it to be shut off, drained and serviced.

The three patterns differ in how the glass is held and how the level is made visible. A tubular gauge leaves a glass tube exposed between its cocks. Reflex and transparent gauges clamp flat, toughened glass into a steel body, which lets them work at far higher pressures and temperatures. The reflex pattern uses the way light behaves at its prism grooves to show the liquid as a dark band, so it reads well even on clear, colourless liquids.

A gauge glass needs no power and is often fitted as the visual check beside an electronic level transmitter. Do not confuse it with a [sight glass](/products/manual-valves/sight-glass/), which shows flow in a pipe. Where the contents are hazardous or the glass would foul quickly, a [magnetic level indicator](/products/level-instruments/magnetic-level-indicator/) is the safer alternative.
