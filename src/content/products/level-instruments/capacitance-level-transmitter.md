faqs:
  - question: Does sticky material on the probe cause errors?
    answer: It can. A coating that clings to the probe as the level falls looks like product that is still there. Probes with build-up compensation, or a mounting where the material drains off cleanly, reduce the problem.
  - question: Why does the probe need setting up for each product?
    answer: The reading depends on the dielectric constant of the contents, which differs between, say, oil and water. The transmitter is calibrated at empty and full, or programmed with the product's properties, so it reads correctly.
  - question: Can the probe be shortened on site?
    answer: Some rod probes can, but it changes the calibration and, on insulated probes, damages the insulation at the cut end. It is better to order the length the tank needs.

A capacitance level transmitter uses the tank contents themselves as part of an electrical capacitor. A metal probe runs down into the tank, and the tank wall, or a second electrode, forms the other plate. Air between them has a low dielectric constant; most liquids and many powders have a higher one. As the level rises around the probe, the capacitance increases in proportion.

The electronics in the head measure this capacitance with a small radio-frequency signal and turn it into a continuous level output. There are no moving parts, and the probe copes with pressure, heat and narrow tanks where a beam would not fit.

For conductive liquids such as water and effluent, the probe is insulated with a coating or sleeve. Uninsulated probes suit oils and dry solids. Probes can be rigid rods or, on tall silos, weighted cables.
