faqs:
  - question: Why does a turbine meter need a strainer?
    answer: The rotor and its bearings have fine clearances. Grit, weld spatter or fibres can jam or wear them, so a strainer upstream is normal practice, especially after new pipework is installed.
  - question: How does viscosity affect a turbine meter?
    answer: Thicker liquids drag on the rotor and change how fast it turns for a given flow. A meter calibrated on water reads differently on a heavier oil, so tell us the liquid and its operating temperature.
  - question: Can it run dry or with air in the line?
    answer: Slugs of air make the rotor overspeed, which wears the bearings and can damage it. Fill the line slowly and keep air out of the meter.
  - question: Will the meter wear out over time?
    answer: The bearings do wear, so readings can drift over the years. Periodic checks against a reference meter catch this before it matters.

A turbine flow meter is one of the oldest and most direct ways to measure flow. A free-spinning rotor with angled blades sits in the stream, held between upstream and downstream supports. The moving fluid pushes on the blades and turns the rotor at a speed proportional to the flow velocity.

A pick-up coil outside the flow path senses each blade as it passes and produces a pulse. The number of pulses gives the total volume, and their frequency gives the flow rate. That pulse output is easy for a counter, batch controller or PLC to use directly.

Turbine meters give very repeatable readings on clean, low-viscosity fluids, and they respond quickly to changes in flow. In return, they need clean fluid, a stable viscosity and some straight pipe ahead of them.
