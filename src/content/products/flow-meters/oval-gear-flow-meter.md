faqs:
  - question: Why is an oval gear meter preferred for diesel and oils?
    answer: It measures volume directly, so its accuracy does not depend on the flow profile, and thicker liquids actually seal the gears better and improve it. Turbine meters lose accuracy as viscosity rises, and electromagnetic meters cannot read oil at all because it does not conduct.
  - question: Does it need straight pipe before and after?
    answer: No. A positive displacement meter is not affected by bends, valves or pumps nearby, which makes it easy to fit on compact skids and tanker pipework.
  - question: Why must a strainer be fitted upstream?
    answer: The gears run with very small clearances. A hard particle can jam or score them, so a fine strainer ahead of the meter is essential, and an air eliminator is added where the line can draw in air.
  - question: Can it be used on water?
    answer: It can, but it is seldom the best choice. Water has little lubricating value, so wear is higher, and other meter types measure water more cheaply.

An oval gear flow meter has two oval gears meshed at right angles inside a close-fitting chamber. Liquid pressure turns the gears, and with every turn each gear sweeps a crescent-shaped pocket of liquid of known volume from inlet to outlet. Counting the turns gives the volume that has passed.

That is positive displacement measurement: the meter does not infer flow from velocity, it parcels the liquid out. The result is high accuracy and repeatability over a wide flow range, and performance that gets better, not worse, as the liquid gets thicker.

Turns are picked up by a mechanical register geared to the rotors, or by magnets in the gears that pulse a sensor for an electronic display and output. The liquid must be clean and free of air, so the meter is always paired with a strainer such as a [basket strainer](/products/manual-valves/basket-strainer/). For thin, clean liquids at higher flows, a [turbine flow meter](/products/flow-meters/turbine-flow-meter/) is the lighter option.
