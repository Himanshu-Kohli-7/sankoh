  - question: Why is a vortex meter popular on steam?
    answer: It has no moving parts to wear in the heat, it reads steam, gas and liquid with the same basic design, and it can be compensated for pressure and temperature to give the steam flow by mass.
  - question: Is there a minimum flow it can read?
    answer: Yes. Below a certain velocity the vortices become too weak to detect and the meter reads zero. Sizing it to the real flow, often smaller than the pipe, keeps the normal flow well inside its range.
  - question: Can pipe vibration cause false readings?
    answer: Strong vibration can look like vortices to the sensor. Good pipe support near the meter and the transmitter's own filtering deal with most cases.
  - question: Does a vortex meter need long straight runs?
    answer: Longer than most other types. Bends, reducers and part-open valves upstream distort the vortex pattern, so we check the upstream layout before recommending one.

A vortex flow meter measures flow by counting swirls. A flat or shaped bar, called a bluff body or shedder, stands across the bore. As fluid passes it, vortices break away from alternate sides, one after another, in a regular pattern known as a Kármán vortex street.

The rate at which those vortices form is proportional to the velocity of the fluid. A sensor behind the bar picks up the tiny pressure pulses they create, and the transmitter turns the pulse frequency into a flow rate and a total.

Because nothing moves and the same principle works for liquids, gases and steam, vortex meters are a common choice on boiler house steam mains and compressed air headers. Adding pressure and temperature inputs lets the transmitter work out mass flow, which is how steam is usually costed.
