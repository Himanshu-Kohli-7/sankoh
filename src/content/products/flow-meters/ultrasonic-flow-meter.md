faqs:
  - question: What is the difference between transit-time and Doppler meters?
    answer: Transit-time meters time sound crossing the pipe and need a fairly clean liquid. Doppler meters bounce sound off particles or bubbles in the stream, so they work on dirty liquids but are less accurate. Most process and water meters use transit time.
  - question: Will a clamp-on meter work on any pipe?
    answer: It works on most metal and plastic pipes, but the wall material, thickness, any internal lining and scale all affect the sound path. Give us the pipe details and we will check that a clamp-on meter can read through it.
  - question: Can it measure gas or compressed air?
    answer: Gas versions exist, but they are a separate design. A meter built for liquids will not read a gas line.
  - question: Why do bubbles upset the reading?
    answer: Air and heavy solids scatter the sound pulses so the transducers lose the signal. Fit the meter away from pump suctions and high points where air gathers.

An ultrasonic flow meter measures liquid flow with sound. Most process meters use the transit-time method: two transducers send pulses diagonally across the pipe, one pointing downstream and one upstream. Sound travelling with the flow arrives a fraction sooner than sound travelling against it, and that time difference gives the velocity of the liquid.

Inline meters carry their transducers in a short measuring tube fitted between flanges. Clamp-on versions strap the transducers to the outside of an existing pipe, so a line can be measured without cutting into it or stopping the process. That makes them handy for surveys and for checking whether another meter is reading true.

The method does not rely on conductivity, so it also measures oils and demineralised water. What it does need is a liquid reasonably free of air and solids, and a steady flow profile in front of it.
