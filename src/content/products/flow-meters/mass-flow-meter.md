  - question: Is a mass flow meter worth the extra cost?

  - question: Can a Coriolis meter measure density as well?
    answer: Yes. The frequency at which the tubes vibrate changes with the density of what is inside them, so most Coriolis transmitters show density and temperature alongside the flow.
  - question: Can a thermal mass meter be used on liquids?
    answer: No. It relies on a gas carrying heat away from a warm sensor, and is meant for air and clean, dry gases of a known make-up.
  - question: Does pipe vibration affect a Coriolis meter?
    answer: It can. Brace the pipework firmly either side of the meter, and keep it away from strong vibration sources such as reciprocating pumps and compressors.

A mass flow meter measures the quantity that matters most in many processes: mass. A volume reading drifts when a liquid warms up or a gas is compressed, but a mass reading does not, so there is nothing to correct for temperature or pressure.

Two principles are common. In a Coriolis meter, the fluid passes through one or two tubes that are made to vibrate. Flow twists the tubes very slightly, and the size of that twist is proportional to the mass passing through. A thermal mass meter, used mainly on gases, heats a sensor in the stream and measures how much heat the moving gas carries away.

Coriolis meters suit liquids and gases where precision justifies the cost. Thermal meters are a lighter, lower-cost choice for compressed air and gas distribution.
