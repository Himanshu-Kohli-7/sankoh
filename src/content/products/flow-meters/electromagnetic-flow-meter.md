faqs:
  - question: Will it measure oil or demineralised water?
    answer: No. The liquid has to conduct electricity above a minimum level for a voltage to appear at the electrodes. Oils, fuels, solvents, gases and very pure water fall below it, so an ultrasonic, turbine or mass meter is the usual choice for those.
  - question: Does the pipe have to run full?
    answer: Yes. A part-filled bore gives a false reading. Fit the meter where the pipe always stays full, such as a rising section or a low point, and keep it away from the top of a line where air collects.
  - question: Why does it need earthing rings on a plastic pipe?
    answer: The measuring signal is tiny and needs a stable reference to the liquid itself. On metal pipe the pipe provides it; on plastic or lined pipe, earthing rings or an earthing electrode do the same job.
  - question: Does it cause a pressure drop?
    answer: Practically none when it is the same size as the pipe, because the bore is clear. If it is sized down to raise the velocity, the reducers add a small loss.

An electromagnetic flow meter, often called a mag meter, measures the flow of any liquid that conducts electricity. Nothing projects into the stream and nothing moves, so it handles dirty water, slurries and corrosive chemicals that would clog or wear other meters.

It works on Faraday's law of induction. Coils in the meter body set up a magnetic field across the pipe. The moving liquid acts as a conductor cutting that field, and a small voltage appears between two electrodes set in the wall. The voltage rises in step with the velocity, and the transmitter turns it into a flow rate and a running total.

An insulating liner stops the signal leaking away through the pipe wall, and both liner and electrodes are chosen to resist the liquid. Changes in density, viscosity or temperature do not affect the reading.
