faqs:
  - question: Paddle or piston flow switch?
    answer: A paddle switch suits larger pipes, from about an inch upwards, and fits into a tee. A piston or shuttle switch sits inline on small pipes and responds to lower flows more precisely. Thermal switches with no moving parts suit dirty or slow-moving liquids.
  - question: Why not use a pressure switch to detect flow?
    answer: Pressure can stay high when a valve downstream is shut or a line is blocked, even though nothing is moving. A flow switch senses the movement itself, which is what a pump or chiller needs to be protected from.
  - question: How is the set point chosen?
    answer: Set it below normal flow but well above zero, so the switch drops out on a real loss of flow and not on normal swings. Most paddle switches have an adjusting screw; on paddles the trip point also depends on the paddle length cut to suit the pipe.
  - question: Does it give the flow rate?
    answer: No. It only reports flow above or below its set point. If you need a reading as well, use a flow meter with an alarm output, or a rotameter with a switch.

A flow switch tells a control circuit whether liquid or air is moving through a pipe. It does not measure how much is flowing. It changes over a contact when the flow rises above, or falls below, a set point, which is enough to stop a pump, block a chiller from starting or raise an alarm.

In a paddle switch, a thin vane sits across the pipe. Flow pushes it aside, and a lever or magnet coupling works a microswitch in the housing outside. In a piston switch, flow lifts a spring-loaded piston that carries a magnet past a sealed reed contact. Thermal switches heat a probe slightly and sense the extra cooling when the fluid moves.

Install it in a straight run of pipe, clear of bends and valves, and with the flow arrow on the body pointing the right way. Where you need a reading as well as an alarm, use a [rotameter](/products/flow-meters/rotameter/) or a meter with a contact output. A [pressure switch](/products/pressure-instruments/pressure-switch/) senses pressure, not movement, and cannot do this job reliably.
