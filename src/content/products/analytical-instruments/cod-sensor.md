faqs:
  - question: What is the difference between COD and BOD?
    answer: COD counts everything a strong chemical oxidant can break down, so it is always the higher of the two and is quick to test. BOD counts only what bacteria can break down, which is closer to what happens in a river or a biological plant, but the test takes days.
  - question: Does the probe need chemical reagents?
    answer: Optical probes do not. They measure light absorption directly in the water, so there is no reagent to buy or waste to dispose of. Automatic analyzers that copy the lab digestion do use reagents.
  - question: Why combine it with a flow meter?
    answer: Concentration alone does not tell you how much pollution leaves the site. Multiplying COD by the flow from a flow meter gives the load, which is what treatment design and discharge limits often depend on.

A COD sensor tracks the chemical oxygen demand of wastewater: the amount of oxygen needed to chemically oxidise the organic matter in it. COD is one of the most common ways to judge the strength of an effluent and the performance of a treatment plant.

In the laboratory, COD is measured by digesting a sample with a strong oxidant at high temperature, which takes a couple of hours. An online probe answers within seconds. The usual design is optical: ultraviolet light passes through a measuring slot open to the water, and the probe measures how much is absorbed. The more dissolved organic matter, the less light gets through; a second wavelength corrects for solids.

Readings are matched to COD through a correlation with lab results on the same wastewater. An automatic wiper or air jet is often fitted to keep the optical windows clean between manual services.
