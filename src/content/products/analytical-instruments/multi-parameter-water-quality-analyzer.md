faqs:
  - question: Is one multi-parameter analyzer better than separate analyzers?
    answer: It saves panel space, wiring and sample pipework, and gives one display and one data link for everything. Separate analyzers make sense where the parameters are measured at different points, or where one sensor needs very different sample conditioning from the others.
  - question: Which parameters can be combined?
    answer: Common sets are pH, ORP, conductivity or TDS, temperature, turbidity, residual chlorine and dissolved oxygen. For effluent, optical sensors can add COD, BOD, TSS and nitrate estimates. Tell us what the permit or process requires and we will suggest a set.
  - question: Do the sensors need a flow cell or can they go in the tank?
    answer: Both are possible. A flow cell on a sample line keeps the sensors together and easy to reach for cleaning. Immersion sensors suit open channels, tanks and basins where there is no convenient sample point.
  - question: How much maintenance does it need?
    answer: Each sensor needs its own care. pH electrodes need regular calibration and replacement, chlorine sensors need periodic electrolyte or membrane changes, and optical sensors need their windows cleaned unless they have automatic wipers.

A multi-parameter water quality analyzer brings several measurements into one system. Instead of a separate transmitter for each parameter, a single controller reads a group of sensors, each measuring one property of the water. Most modern systems use digital sensors on a shared cable, so a parameter can be added later by plugging in another sensor.

The sensors sit together in a flow cell fed from a sample line, or hang directly in a tank or channel. The controller shows every value on one screen, raises alarms when a limit is crossed, and passes the readings to a PLC, SCADA system or remote server.

The individual measurements work as they do in a single analyzer, so the choice of each sensor still depends on the water.
