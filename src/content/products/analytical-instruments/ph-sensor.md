faqs:
  - question: How often does a pH sensor need calibrating?
    answer: It depends on the liquid. Clean water may need checking every few weeks; dirty effluent or hot process liquid more often. Calibration uses two or more buffer solutions of known pH, and a regular routine catches drift before it matters.
  - question: How long does the electrode last?
    answer: The glass ages with use, and the reference slowly loses its fill or becomes poisoned. Life varies widely with temperature, chemistry and cleaning, so treat the electrode as a consumable and keep a spare for critical loops.
  - question: Can the sensor be left dry?
    answer: No. The glass bulb must stay moist to respond properly, so keep the protective cap filled with storage solution when it is out of the process. A sensor that has dried out may recover after soaking, or may need replacing.
  - question: What mounting options are there?
    answer: Sensors go into a pipe through an inline fitting, into a tank on an immersion pipe, or into a flow cell fed with a sample stream. Retractable fittings let the sensor be pulled out for cleaning while the line keeps running.

A pH sensor measures how acidic or alkaline a liquid is. It is the probe end of a pH measuring loop: the sensor sits in the water or process liquid, and a pH transmitter or controller reads its signal and shows the result.

Most sensors combine two electrodes in one body. The measuring electrode ends in a thin bulb of special glass that develops a voltage depending on the hydrogen ion activity of the liquid. The reference electrode provides a steady voltage to compare it against, through a small junction in contact with the sample. The difference between them changes by a known amount per pH unit.

That relationship shifts with temperature, so many sensors include a temperature element for automatic compensation. The glass and junction slowly age and can be fouled by oils, solids or scale, which is why pH sensors are cleaned and calibrated regularly.
