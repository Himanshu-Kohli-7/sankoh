howToChoose:
  - title: Start with the job
    text: Decide what the valve has to do. Ball, butterfly and gate valves isolate a line. Globe and needle valves regulate flow. Check valves stop reverse flow. Safety valves relieve excess pressure.
  - title: Know the media
    text: Clean liquid, gas, steam, slurry or a corrosive chemical each call for different body and seat materials, and sometimes a different valve type. A knife gate valve, for example, suits slurries that would jam an ordinary gate valve.
  - title: Check pressure and temperature
    text: Note the normal working values and the highest the line can reach. Together they decide the pressure rating and the seat and seal materials.
  - title: Match the size and end connection
    text: The valve has to fit the pipework you have – the line size and whether the ends are flanged, screwed, wafer or welded.
  - title: Think about how it is operated
    text: Quarter-turn valves such as ball and butterfly valves open and close quickly. Multi-turn valves such as gate and globe valves are slower but give finer control. If a valve is operated often or from a distance, consider an actuated valve instead.
faqs:
  - question: Which manual valve is best for on/off isolation?
    answer: Ball valves and butterfly valves are the usual choice for quick quarter-turn isolation, and gate valves for a straight, full-bore passage. The best fit depends on your line size, media, pressure and temperature, so send us those details and we will recommend one.
  - question: What is the difference between a gate valve and a globe valve?
    answer: A gate valve lifts a gate out of the flow path. It is meant to be fully open or fully closed and gives very little resistance when open. A globe valve moves a plug against a seat, which makes it suited to regulating flow, at the cost of a higher pressure drop.
  - question: What is the difference between a ball valve and a butterfly valve?
    answer: Both are quarter-turn valves. A ball valve seals with a bored ball and gives a tight shut-off, and is common on smaller lines. A butterfly valve uses a disc that stays in the flow path. It is lighter and more compact, which is why it is often chosen for larger pipe sizes.

Manual valves are operated by hand, with a lever, handwheel or gear, and need no air or power supply. They start, stop, regulate or protect the flow of liquids, gases and steam in a pipeline, which makes them the most common valves in any plant: on water and utility lines, steam and condensate, compressed air, process fluids and slurries.

The range covers every job a hand-operated valve does. Ball, butterfly, gate, knife gate, piston and flush bottom valves isolate a line or a tank. Globe and needle valves regulate flow. Non-return (check) valves stop reverse flow. Safety valves relieve excess pressure. Strainers, sight glasses and steam traps protect and monitor the line.
