howToChoose:
  - title: Decide who or what needs the reading
    text: A dial gauge serves the operator standing next to it and needs no wiring. A sensor wired to a controller, recorder or PLC suits automatic control. A transmitter is added when the signal has to travel a long way or must reach the system as a standard current loop.
  - title: Fit the sensor to the temperature band
    text: Resistance sensors give steady, precise readings across the everyday process band. Hotter places, such as furnaces, kilns, flue ducts and exhausts, call for sensors built for heat. Give us the normal and the highest temperature the tip will see.
  - title: Get the insertion length right
    text: The tip must sit in the moving fluid, not in a dead pocket near the wall, or the reading lags and runs low. Tell us the pipe or vessel size and where the tapping is, so the stem length can be chosen to reach the right depth.
  - title: Protect the sensor from the process
    text: A thermowell lets a gauge or sensor be removed without draining the line and shields it from pressure, flow and corrosive media. It slows the response slightly, which matters only where temperature changes very fast.
  - title: Check the head, connection and surroundings
    text: Note the thread or flange on the tapping and the cable entry. Say if the head faces rain, hosing down or a flammable atmosphere, since each needs a suitable enclosure. Long cable runs and electrical noise favour a transmitter close to the sensor.
faqs:
  - question: What is the difference between an RTD and a thermocouple?
    answer: An RTD changes its electrical resistance with temperature and is prized for stable, repeatable readings at moderate temperatures. A thermocouple produces a small voltage from the junction of two different metals and is the usual choice where it gets much hotter. Each needs an input or transmitter made for its type.
  - question: Do I need a thermowell?
    answer: On any pressurised line, or where the sensor may need changing while the plant runs, yes. It is also worth fitting on fast-flowing or corrosive service. A bare probe is fine only in open tanks, ducts or low-pressure air.
  - question: Why does my temperature reading respond slowly?
    answer: Usually because of a thick or oversized thermowell, an air gap between the sensor and the bore of the well, or a tip that does not reach the flow. Heat-transfer paste in the well and the right insertion length both help.
  - question: Can a dial gauge send a signal to a PLC?
    answer: A plain mechanical gauge cannot. Choose a sensor with a transmitter for that, or fit both on separate tappings so operators keep a local dial and the control system gets its own signal.

Temperature instruments show or signal how hot a process is, so that it can be watched, controlled and kept safe. A [temperature gauge](/products/temperature-instruments/temperature-gauge-mechanical/) gives an on-the-spot dial reading with no power. An [RTD sensor](/products/temperature-instruments/rtd-sensor/) or a [high temperature sensor](/products/temperature-instruments/high-temperature-sensor/) turns heat into an electrical signal for a controller. A [temperature transmitter](/products/temperature-instruments/temperature-transmitter/) converts that signal into a standard output that travels cleanly to a PLC or control room.

A [thermowell](/products/temperature-instruments/thermowell/) completes most installations. It is a closed tube welded or screwed into the line, so the gauge or sensor inside can be withdrawn without draining or depressurising anything.

You will find these instruments on boilers and steam lines, heat exchangers, ovens and dryers, chilled water plants, HVAC and building automation, and across industrial automation.
