howToChoose:
  - title: Name the parameter and why you need it
    text: Dosing control, a discharge record and a quick operator check each call for different accuracy and response. Say whether the reading will drive a pump or blower, feed a report, or simply warn someone.
  - title: Describe the sample honestly
    text: Clean RO permeate, raw river water and oily effluent need very different sensors. Mention solids, oil, grease, fibres, scale-forming salts, temperature and anything aggressive in the water, since these decide the sensor and how often it needs cleaning.
  - title: Choose how the sensor meets the water
    text: Immersion probes hang in tanks and channels. Inline fittings put the sensor straight into a pipe. Flow cells take a small sample stream to a panel, where several sensors can be grouped and serviced together.
  - title: Plan calibration and cleaning from the start
    text: Every analytical sensor drifts or fouls in time. Allow access for routine cleaning and calibration, and consider automatic cleaning such as wipers or air jets on dirty water. Optical BOD and COD probes also need a correlation with lab results.
  - title: Match the transmitter and the signal
    text: Check that the sensor and transmitter belong together, and that the output, relays and power supply suit your panel or PLC. One multi-parameter transmitter can sometimes serve several sensors.
faqs:
  - question: What is the difference between a sensor and an analyzer?
    answer: The sensor is the part in contact with the water. An analyzer is the complete measuring system, including the transmitter or controller that reads the sensor, shows the value and sends it on. A sensor alone needs a matching transmitter to be useful.
  - question: Can online sensors replace laboratory tests?
    answer: They complement them. Online sensors show changes as they happen, between samples and at night, while lab tests remain the reference, especially for BOD and COD. Regular comparison keeps both in agreement.
  - question: Which parameters matter most for an effluent treatment plant?
    answer: pH for neutralisation, dissolved oxygen for aeration, and COD or BOD with flow for load and discharge. Turbidity or suspended solids show how well settling is working. Requirements differ by plant and consent, so check yours.
  - question: How often do these sensors need attention?
    answer: It depends on the water. Clean water may need little more than a periodic calibration, while raw effluent can foul a sensor within days without automatic cleaning. Plan a routine to match the site.

Analytical instruments measure what is in water, not just how much of it there is or how hot it is. They tell operators whether water is fit to drink, to feed a boiler or to discharge, and they let treatment plants dose chemicals and run blowers to match the real load.

A [pH sensor](/products/analytical-instruments/ph-sensor/) and [pH transmitter](/products/analytical-instruments/ph-transmitter/) track acidity for neutralisation and dosing. Online [BOD](/products/analytical-instruments/bod-sensor/) and [COD sensors](/products/analytical-instruments/cod-sensor/) follow the organic load in effluent between laboratory tests. A [conductivity and TDS analyzer](/products/analytical-instruments/conductivity-tds-analyzer/) watches dissolved salts in RO plants, boilers and cooling towers. A [dissolved oxygen analyzer](/products/analytical-instruments/dissolved-oxygen-do-analyzer/) controls aeration, and a [turbidity analyzer](/products/analytical-instruments/turbidity-analyzer/) checks that filtration is working.
